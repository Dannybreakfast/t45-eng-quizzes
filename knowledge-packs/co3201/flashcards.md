# CO3201 Flashcards

**Q:** For a heading change of less than 30 degrees, what bank angle is used?  
**A:** A bank angle that equals the heading change  
> For turns of less than 30 degrees of heading change, use a bank angle that equals that change.

**Q:** A heading change from 020 to 045 degrees is required. What bank angle?  
**A:** 25 degrees  
> For example, if a heading change from 020 degrees to 045 degrees were required (a 25-degree change), you would use a bank angle of 25 degrees.

**Q:** If the heading change is 30 degrees or greater, what bank angle is used?  
**A:** 30 degrees  
> If the heading change is 30 degrees or greater, use a bank angle of 30 degrees.

**Q:** Prior to entering a normal turn you should be:  
**A:** In straight-and-level flight with trim properly set  
> Prior to entering the turn, you should be in straight-and-level flight with trim properly set.

**Q:** As you roll into the turn you lose some vertical component of lift, so you must:  
**A:** Add power and aft stick (trim) to compensate  
> As you roll into the turn, you will lose some of the vertical component of lift, so you'll have to add power and aft stick (trim) to compensate.

**Q:** What lead point is used for initiating the roll-out?  
**A:** Approximately 10% of the angle of bank  
> Use a lead point of approximately 10% of the angle of bank for initiating your roll-out.

**Q:** As you roll out of the turn the aircraft has a tendency to:  
**A:** Climb, because the vertical component of lift increases  
> As you roll out of the turn, the vertical component of lift will increase and the aircraft will have a tendency to climb.

**Q:** To counteract the climb tendency on roll-out you:  
**A:** Lower the nose to level flight reference and reduce power to the pre-turn setting  
> To counteract this, lower the nose attitude back to level flight reference and reduce power to the pre-turn setting.

**Q:** Any aft stick or trim added during the turn must:  
**A:** Come out when the turn is complete  
> Any aft stick or trim added during the turn will have to come out when the turn is complete.

**Q:** Normal turns summary — Throttle:  
**A:** Increase as needed to maintain airspeed  
> Throttle: Increase as needed to maintain airspeed

**Q:** Normal turns summary — Pitch:  
**A:** As required to maintain level flight  
> Pitch: As required to maintain level flight

**Q:** Normal turns summary — Lead point for roll-out:  
**A:** 10% AOB  
> Lead point for roll-out: 10% AOB

**Q:** Using the 10% AOB lead, roll-out from a 30-degree AOB turn begins about how many degrees before the desired heading?  
**A:** 3 degrees  
> Use a lead point of approximately 10% of the angle of bank for initiating your roll-out.

**Q:** The turn pattern consists of:  
**A:** Three pairs of left and right turns  
> Consisting of three pairs of left and right turns, the turn pattern gives you practice in smoothly performing a series of linked turns at a constant altitude and airspeed.

**Q:** Turn pattern, first set: 30-degree AOB turns for how much heading change each?  
**A:** 60 degrees  
> The turn consists of two 30-degree angle of bank turns for 60 degrees of heading change each, two 45-degree AOB turns for 90 degrees of heading change each, and two 60-degree AOB turns for 120 degrees of heading change each.

**Q:** Turn pattern, second set: 45-degree AOB turns for how much heading change each?  
**A:** 90 degrees  
> The turn consists of two 30-degree angle of bank turns for 60 degrees of heading change each, two 45-degree AOB turns for 90 degrees of heading change each, and two 60-degree AOB turns for 120 degrees of heading change each.

**Q:** Turn pattern, third set: 60-degree AOB turns for how much heading change each?  
**A:** 120 degrees  
> The turn consists of two 30-degree angle of bank turns for 60 degrees of heading change each, two 45-degree AOB turns for 90 degrees of heading change each, and two 60-degree AOB turns for 120 degrees of heading change each.

**A:** 30/60, 45/90, 60/120  
> The turn consists of two 30-degree angle of bank turns for 60 degrees of heading change each, two 45-degree AOB turns for 90 degrees of heading change each, and two 60-degree AOB turns for 120 degrees of heading change each.

**Q:** How do you set 45 degrees of bank on the ADI?  
**A:** Place the bank angle pointer halfway between the 30- and 60-degree scale markers  
> Since there are no 45-degree bank angle scale markers on the ADI display, you must place the bank angle pointer halfway between the 30-degree and 60-degree bank angle scale markers.

**Q:** Turn reversals in the turn pattern are executed:  
**A:** Smoothly, with no straight-and-level legs

**Q:** At the end of the turn pattern you:  
**A:** Recover wings level on original heading  
> At the end of the maneuver, recover wings level on original heading.

**A:** More aft stick and more power

**Q:** When reversing turns, to avoid gaining altitude you:

**Q:** A 1/2 standard-rate turn is performed at what rate?  
**A:** 1-1/2 degrees per second  
> A 1/2 standard-rate turn (1/2 SRT) is performed at 1-1/2 degrees per second.

**Q:** At 1/2 SRT, a 30-degree heading change takes:  
**A:** 20 seconds  
> Therefore, a heading change of 30 degrees will take 20 seconds to complete.

**Q:** To achieve the 1/2 SRT rate at different airspeeds you must:  
**A:** Vary the bank angle  
> To accomplish this rate at different airspeeds, you will have to vary the bank angle.

**Q:** Rule of thumb for 1/2 SRT bank angle between 15,000 and 20,000 ft:  
**A:** Approximately 10 percent of indicated airspeed  
> A good rule of thumb for determining bank angle between 15,000 and 20,000 ft is to use approximately 10 percent of indicated airspeed.

**Q:** The 10%-of-KIAS rule of thumb applies between which altitudes?  
**A:** 15,000 and 20,000 ft  
> A good rule of thumb for determining bank angle between 15,000 and 20,000 ft is to use approximately 10 percent of indicated airspeed.

**Q:** At higher altitudes, a 1/2 SRT requires:  
**A:** More bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed  
> At higher altitudes, you will need more bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed, while at lower altitudes you will need less bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed to maintain a 1/2 SRT.

**Q:** At lower altitudes, a 1/2 SRT requires:  
**A:** Less bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed  
> At higher altitudes, you will need more bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed, while at lower altitudes you will need less bank than 10 percent of indicated airspeed to maintain a 1/2 SRT.

**Q:** At 250 KIAS and 18,000 ft, 1/2 SRT bank angle is approximately:

**Q:** To establish and maintain a 1/2 SRT at any altitude, adjust bank to achieve:  
**A:** One needle-width of deflection on the turn needle

**Q:** The power setting for increasing airspeed depends on:  
**A:** The magnitude of the desired change

**Q:** For an airspeed increase of less than 20 kts, you:  
**A:** Advance the throttle beyond the setting for the new airspeed, then reduce to the approximate setting to maintain it

**Q:** For airspeed increases greater than 20 kts, advance the power to:  
**A:** MRT

**Q:** Speed increase greater than 20 kts: at what lead do you reduce the throttle?

**A:** Reduce power below the requirement for the desired airspeed, then at a 5-kt lead advance power as required  
> To perform normal airspeed decreases, reduce the power below the power requirement for the desired airspeed, allowing the airspeed to decrease, and then at a lead point of 5 kts, advance power as required for the new airspeed.

**Q:** Lead point for advancing power in a normal airspeed decrease:  
**A:** 5 kts  
> ...and then at a lead point of 5 kts, advance power as required for the new airspeed.

**Q:** As airspeed decreases, to maintain level flight you need:  
**A:** More nose up trim

**Q:** For large or rapid airspeed decreases you:  
**A:** Reduce the throttle to idle and fully extend the speed brakes  
> When making large or rapid airspeed decreases, reduce the throttle to idle and fully extend the speed brakes.

**Q:** When you extend the speed brakes, expect:  
**A:** A pitch up, and retrim to maintain altitude  
> When you extend the speed brakes, expect a pitch up and anticipate the need to retrim the aircraft to maintain altitude.

**Q:** In a rapid speed decrease, at what lead do you retract speed brakes and reset power?  
**A:** 5 kts  
> Because the airspeed will rapidly decrease, you will need to use a 5-kt lead point to retract the speed brakes and reset the power to avoid undershooting your desired airspeed.

**Q:** When you retract the speed brakes, anticipate:  
**A:** A pitch down  
> Anticipate a pitch down when you retract the speed brakes.

**Q:** Plan your descent to be in level flight at the MDA:  
**A:** Prior to reaching the MAP  
> Plan your descent to be in level flight at the MDA prior to reaching the MAP.

**Q:** Maximum descent rate from the FAF (TACAN):  
**A:** 1,000 fpm

**Q:** Keep heading changes small so you don't chase:

> Keep heading changes small so you don't chase the final approach course on the HSI display or the course situation steering arrow on the HUD.

**A:** About 300 pph

**Q:** The two basic types of ground-controlled approaches are:  
**A:** PAR and ASR  
> There are two basic types of ground-controlled approaches—the precision radar approach (PAR) and the surveillance radar approach (ASR).

**Q:** A PAR provides:  
**A:** Precise course, glidepath, and range information (precision approach)  
> A PAR provides you with precise course, glidepath, and range information and is classified as a precision approach.

**Q:** An ASR provides:  
**A:** Lower resolution course and range information only (no glideslope); non-precision  
> An ASR (commonly referred to as a surveillance approach) provides lower resolution course and range information only (no glideslope) and is classified as a non-precision approach.

**Q:** Glideslope is defined as:  
**A:** The descent angle assigned to an approach to a given runway for obstacle clearance and/or signal reception  
> Glideslope is defined as the descent angle assigned to an approach to a given runway for obstacle clearance and/or signal reception.

**Q:** Glidepath is defined as:  
**A:** The portion of a precision approach that intercepts the azimuth of an ILS approach or the FAC of a PAR approach  
> Glidepath is defined as the portion of a precision approach that intercepts the azimuth of an ILS approach or the FAC of a PAR approach.

**Q:** PAR and ASR approaches are divided into which two segments?  
**A:** Initial pattern and final approach  
> Both the PAR and ASR approaches are divided into two segments, initial pattern and final approach.

**Q:** During the initial pattern of an ASR or PAR you are guided by:  
**A:** Surveillance radar  
> During the initial pattern of an ASR or PAR approach, you will be guided by surveillance radar.

**Q:** The initial pattern segment ends when the aircraft is inbound on the FAC at approximately:  
**A:** 8 nm from touchdown  
> This segment includes all maneuvering up to the point at which your aircraft is inbound on the final approach course and at approximately 8 nm from touchdown.

**Q:** During the transition to final, the GCA controller directs:  
**A:** Your headings and altitudes  
> During the transition to final, the GCA controller will direct your headings and altitudes.

**Q:** Controller instructions to initiate turns and descents should be complied with:  
**A:** Immediately  
> All controller instructions to initiate turns and descents should be complied with immediately.

**Q:** In the GCA pattern, turns are:  
**A:** Standard rate, not to exceed 30 degrees AOB  
> In the pattern, maintain standard rate turns not to exceed 30 degrees AOB.

**Q:** On final, AOB should approximate the degrees to be turned, not to exceed:  
**A:** Half standard rate (approximately 10 degrees AOB)  
> On final, your AOB should approximate the number of degrees to be turned not to exceed half standard rate (approximately 10 degrees AOB).

**Q:** Good technique for AOB turning base to final:  
**A:** 20 degrees  
> A good technique is 30 degrees AOB in pattern, 20 degrees AOB turning base to final.

**Q:** Good technique for AOB in the GCA pattern:  
**A:** 30 degrees  
> A good technique is 30 degrees AOB in pattern, 20 degrees AOB turning base to final.

**Q:** Once on final, max AOB for heading corrections to course:  
**A:** 10 degrees  
> Once on final, do not exceed 10 degrees AOB for heading corrections to course.

**Q:** Radar approach minimums can be found:  
**A:** In FLIP instrument approach procedure publications — on the approach plates and in the radar approach minimums section  
> Radar approach minimums can be found in the FLIP instrument approach procedure publications, both on the approach plates and in the radar approach minimums section (Figure 6-29).

**Q:** Absolute minimums when single-piloted:  
**A:** 200-1/2  
> Although some published approach minimums are lower than 200-1/2, you are limited to absolute minimums of 200-1/2 when single-piloted.

**Q:** During a GCA, the controller continuously feeds you:  
**A:** Course and heading information, and on a PAR, glidepath information on final  
> During a GCA, your position in the pattern and on final will be directed by a controller who will continuously feed you course and heading information and, in the case of a PAR approach, glidepath information on final.
